Health Translator: Plain-Language Care Portal Concept

Secondary ResearchPrimary ResearchInterviewsSurveysTrust-Centered DesignInformation ArchitectureMultimodal InteractionPlain Language
TL;DR

Collaborative design concept. The team produced secondary research, participant research, interface flows, and an AI-assistance proposal. It did not deploy a clinical translation system or establish HIPAA compliance. The design centers on patient control and an always-visible way to reach a person.

I contributed to this team project. The report documents shared research, storyboards, prototype testing, and a human-escalation concept without assigning individual tasks or decisions. I present those as team results, not work I completed alone.

The Problem

Medical records and patient portal content are written at a graduate reading level. The average American adult reads at an 8th-grade level. Millions of patients receive test results, diagnosis summaries, and care instructions they cannot parse. They log into portals, see clinical language, and either guess at meaning, give up, or wait to ask a doctor at a follow-up that may be weeks away.

Health literacy is a social determinant of health. Poor health literacy correlates with worse outcomes, lower medication adherence, and higher rates of preventable hospitalization. The portal often makes this worse.

Our team set out to design an AI plain-language translation layer for healthcare portals: something that converts clinical language to plain language on request.

The Context

The team's September 2025 secondary-research report cited a patient-portal market estimate of $3.5 billion in 2023 and more than $19 billion by 2033, along with widespread hospital portal availability. Sources are listed below.

The assignment also reviewed the federal HTI-1 Final Rule and HIPAA as implementation constraints.

A cited study of 8,000 patients found that more than 95 percent wanted continued immediate access to results through a portal.

The Research

We ran interviews and surveys with patients and healthcare users. We wanted to hear directly what was frustrating, what was confusing, and what would feel safe to use.

Participants knew the language was technical. They were frustrated that understanding felt out of reach.

We also heard consistent anxiety about the portal itself: about what records they might find, about whether they would be notified before their doctor called, about reading a value without knowing if it was normal. The AI translation concept landed well in research sessions, but only when participants understood what it was doing and felt they could override or question it.

The Design Challenge: Trust

In low-stakes contexts like music recommendations, search autocomplete, or email drafts, AI errors are annoying. In healthcare, an AI that mistranslates a lab result or softens language about a serious finding can cause harm. Participants in our research understood this. Their skepticism about AI in healthcare matched the stakes.

The system had to be clear about what it was doing and about its limitations, and built on one principle: the patient is always in control, and they can always reach a human.

What We Designed

The concept placed an optional translation layer inside a patient portal without replacing or obscuring the clinical record. The proposed flow kept the original text visible and let a patient request a plain-language version.

Six design domains guided the proposal: plain-language explanations, user control, error recovery, limited personalization, WCAG-aligned accessibility, and a visible path to human support. HIPAA, handling of protected health information, clinical accuracy, and institutional review were outside the project.

  • On-demand plain-language translation: converts clinical text to accessible language when requested, without overwriting or hiding the source record
  • Audio reading: translated content can be read aloud, extending access to patients with low literacy, visual impairment, or who simply prefer listening
  • Persistent human escalation: a visible, always-available path to a care coordinator or support staff member, present in every state of the interface, whether or not the AI has failed
  • Transparency about AI limitations: the system is explicit that translations are AI-generated, that clinical decisions should involve a provider, and that accuracy cannot be guaranteed for every term
  • Reading level indicator: patients can see what level the plain-language output targets, making the translation itself legible

Offering text and audio from the start serves a broader range of users without requiring anyone to self-identify as needing a different format.

What We Learned

The language gap

Users who struggled with clinical language were not struggling to understand their health situation. They were struggling with the language the institution had chosen to write in. A translation tool that treats users as capable adults facing an unnecessarily opaque system is a different product from one that treats them as not smart enough. We tried to build the first.

Working on a team

Working on a team surfaced assumptions I did not know I was making. Another team member pushed back on a design decision about when to show the escalation option, and was right.
