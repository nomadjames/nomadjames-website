01 / THE IDEA

The library remembers files. I wanted it to remember relationships.

DJ software is good at storing tracks and weak at helping a person recover the connections between them. I wanted to choose one track I already knew, then ask what else in the archive could sit near it by tempo, key, credit, release, or scene. Related research: the Ableton UX evaluation suite examines expert music software through several evaluation methods.

03 / WHAT IS PUBLIC

What the public index contains.

The archive holds 51,302 files. Of those, 47,445 were viable for the public index.

Each track carries artist, title, album, genre, year, label, the BPM and key from each DJ program, a summary of its key by section, and a section timeline where one exists. The index contains no audio, file paths, filenames, play history, crates, comments, or private IDs.

04 / THE PLAYER

I built a radio to find the tunes I forgot I owned.

After over 30 years of collecting, recall is the problem. Shuffle cannot follow a musical thread, and DJ software mostly returns what I remember to search for. Atlas Radio walks my own library by sound, choosing the next track, blending it on the beat, and recording why it made that choice. I built the iPhone and Mac apps with Claude Code in one day.

THE WAVEFORM

Serato colors for every track.

The player draws each track the way Serato does: lows in red, mids in green, highs in blue. A kick, a vocal and a hi-hat show up as different colors, so I can read the next track before it plays. A short Python script builds them. It decodes each file with ffmpeg, cuts it into 400 slices, and measures the energy below 250 Hz, from 250 Hz to 2.5 kHz, and above 2.5 kHz. All three bands share one scale, so the color balance holds. The script skips finished tracks, so it can stop and pick up again. Reading 50,000 files off a spinning drive was the slow part, so my Mac and my Linux workstation each started from one end of the list. The app draws bar lines, phrase lines, hot cues and the shaded blend zone on top.

THE BLEND

Two decks, one downbeat.

Beat grids from Serato, with Rekordbox as fallback, line up the incoming track with the outgoing one. At the downbeat, the outgoing track takes the incoming tempo while it fades. The beats stayed within 1 ms through the blend in tests. Three silent gaps forced three different fixes: App Nap stalled a timer-driven fade, so the fade moved into the audio timeline; repeated playback-rate writes restarted audio output, so tempo changes happen once at the downbeat; and the phone needed time to buffer, so only a preloaded blend skips the wait.

The run report explains every pick: where the candidate sat among its neighbors, what it shared with the previous tracks, the key and tempo move, and how I rated the transition. I can trace a surprising choice back to the decision that made it.

Atlas Radio is a private app with no public download. It uses its own audio-similarity index, separate from the public index above. The embedding model has a research-only license, so this is not a commercial product.

05 / MODEL FIELD NOTES

Testing audio models on my own archive.

I tested four things on the same workstation: whether a model recognizes a track from a different section, tempo against DJ metadata, classifier scores, and structure analysis from separated stems.

I want a repeatable, AI-assisted way to analyze electronic music. The goal is to help people understand what tracks are doing and use that while writing the music they want. I test each part on my own archive because I know it.

Finding related tracks is the first test. A useful neighboring track gives a DJ or musician a concrete reference for groove, texture, arrangement, energy, or production choices. Some matches come from facts: tempo, key, and shared artists, labels, versions, and releases. Others come from embedding models, which place tracks near each other by how they sound. Before relying on those models for deeper analysis, I needed to know whether their output stayed stable on this material.

MILESTONE / FULL-LIBRARY AUDIO RETRIEVAL

The archive can now suggest a playlist.

The fingerprinting run covered 51,304 audio files. A fingerprint here is a list of 1,280 numbers that describes how a track sounds. The run produced 51,249 usable 1,280-dimensional fingerprints and excluded 55 files. A second decoding pass recovered 160 files that the first one missed.

The full-archive benchmark used 32 fixed queries at ten results each. Audio search took 109.17 milliseconds at the 95th percentile. It returned tracks by the same artist at 10.219 times the chance rate. Audio and metadata rankings overlapped on only 10.625 percent of results.

I then used the search to build playlists. Candidate tracks needed a usable fingerprint, a private history signal, a tempo from 120 to 134 BPM, a duration from three to nine minutes, and at least one techno, house, or bassline tag from the model. The transition score favored closeness in sound to the previous track, then closeness to the starting track, a compatible key, a small tempo change, shared tags, and the history signal.

I built the sequencing code with AI assistance. The code picks by score, and I can read why. A beam search compared hundreds of partial sequences, blocked repeated tracks and artists, and aimed for the requested length. The one-hour test used ten artists and landed at 60 minutes 39 seconds. Median adjacent sonic similarity was 88.7 percent, the weakest link was 83.7 percent, and the largest tempo move was 1.01 BPM. Six of nine transitions had compatible key evidence.

The five-hour stress test used 49 artists and landed at five hours and two seconds. Median adjacent similarity was 86.5 percent, the weakest link was 80.2 percent, and the largest tempo move was 3.3 BPM. Thirty-nine of 48 transitions had compatible key evidence.

The first two mixes sounded good in the car. Mix 2 reused all ten Mix 1 tracks because I treated the longer request as an extension. Numbered mixes now exclude every earlier track unless I ask for a reprise.

Mix 3 pushed farther out without forcing impossible changes. It used 34 tracks, landed at three hours and one second, moved from 118 to 140 BPM, and crossed 15 normalized key positions. Its 33 transitions used 16 adjacent, seven relative, seven two-step, and three diagonal Camelot moves. The largest direct tempo change was 4.01 BPM, and the weakest adjacent audio match was 71.1 percent. It shared no tracks or normalized artist-title identities with the first two mixes.

Mix 4 held house as the floor and pushed the psychedelic proxy instead. It used 30 tracks and landed within a fraction of a second of three hours. Every track carried a house label among its top three model tags. The route stayed between 121 and 129 BPM, never moved more than 2 BPM at once, crossed 12 key positions, and held a 77.2 percent minimum adjacent audio match. The proxy is a score that rewards acid, dub, tribal, progressive, leftfield, and experimental tags.

The four decision images were generated with GPT Image 2.5 Sunburst through Codex OAuth. The captions and the matching infographic were added in code so the labels are exact. I directed this work, and it was implemented with Boddicker, my AI collaborator.

All four playlists were imported into Apple's Music app and checked against the original track order, track count, and duration.

The remaining test still happens in the car. This ranking does not hear phrase structure, vocal clashes, loudness jumps, or whether I would actually make the transition.

MILESTONE / SECTIONAL KEY AND ARRANGEMENT

Key by section, across the whole archive.

This scan covered the full archive. The tests further down used a 100-track sample. The scan produced 51,178 complete tonal maps across 519,685 analyzed sections, with zero unresolved failures. A tonal map lists the predicted key for each section of a track.

Only 25.0 percent of the 51,178 mapped tracks held one predicted key across every analyzed section. Separately, 72.7 percent were flagged for closer review. This does not show that the tracks themselves change key. It shows that one whole-track key label does not match the section-by-section predictions for most tracks.

I compared two key models, MusicalKeyCNN and Madmom, on two separate groups of tracks. In 10,000 tracks with unstable keys, the two models agreed exactly on 5,139, or 51.39 percent. In a separate 2,000 tracks with stable keys, they agreed on 1,492, or 74.6 percent, with zero failures.

That split supports sorting by confidence: stable tracks can take a faster path while unstable ones get closer review. Agreement between two models is not accuracy.

The arrangement pilot covered 100 tracks, and all 100 completed with zero errors. It proposed 985 segments, used 305 beat-grid entries from DJ software, and made 243 grid comparisons. The beat grids came from Rekordbox, Serato, and Traktor where available and were compared with timing from Madmom.

The segment boundaries and role names are machine guesses. Madmom outputs are research-only under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 pending licensing review. An archive-wide Madmom key rerun is not recommended.

As of September 2026, the next step is three reference tracks analyzed in depth by a person, then ten, then twenty. Three establishes the method. Ten tests repeatability. Twenty can form a small reference set for evaluation. Twenty cannot train a capable generative model from scratch.

The reference maps will be made in Ableton before the machine maps are consulted, to reduce anchoring. They will capture bars and timestamps, section function, layers, rhythm, harmony, energy, transition devices, production techniques, uncertainty, and how to recreate each technique.

The long-term goal is an analysis tool.A user drops in a track and receives its structure, key by section, rhythm, energy contour, layer schedule, transition devices, probable production techniques, the uncertainty in each, and guidance on recreating the techniques. Related tracks would serve as concrete references. This tool does not exist yet.
LOCAL MODEL TRAINING

Two training passes. Nothing promoted.

I built and ran an AI-assisted experiment to test whether a small local language model could produce structured musical analysis. Neither fine-tuned model was reliable enough to keep.

I generated 88,373 training examples from 24,656 tracks. I set aside 2,048 examples for training and 90 for evaluation, divided evenly across three tasks: interpreting key by section, arrangement boundaries, and arrangement roles. The model, Qwen2.5 3B Instruct, ran locally with 4-bit QLoRA, a low-memory fine-tuning method, on one RTX 2080 SUPER.

The untuned model returned valid strict JSON on 71.1 percent of the held-out cases and matched none of the expected outputs exactly. The first pass used 100 optimizer steps and trained on each prompt and answer together. Exact match rose to 21.1 percent, and tonal-decision macro F1 rose from 0.700 to 0.907. Strict JSON fell to 66.7 percent, below the untuned model, so I rejected it.

The second pass used 200 optimizer steps and completion-only loss, which focused training on the answer. Exact match rose to 22.2 percent, strict JSON recovered to 70.0 percent, and arrangement-role macro F1 reached 0.400. The boundary-decision score remained 0.0. Because valid output still trailed the untuned model, I rejected this pass too.

More steps and the answer-only loss recovered most of the lost formatting but not all of it. The tonal task improved. The arrangement-role task improved a little. The arrangement-boundary task did not improve.

Before I train again, the arrangement tasks need reference analyses made by people, so machine labels can be measured against them.

THE FIRST TEST

Folder labels could not answer the question.

The first experiment took one thirty-second excerpt from each of 100 tracks and ranked fifteen embedding models by whether a track's five nearest neighbors came from the same broad source category. Those categories came from folder organization, not from listening judgments.

Random precision at five was 0.091. The highest observed score was 0.304. Every model had a negative silhouette score, which means those categories did not form separated clusters in any embedding space.

That test can describe rough folder alignment. It cannot decide whether a retrieved track is musically useful.

The labels were weak.The next test used the one identity label I could verify without interpretation: two excerpts came from the same recording.
THE PAIRED-SECTION TEST

Can a model recognize the same track from a different section?

I made View A and View B from the same 100 private tracks. Each view contained a separate thirty-second excerpt. All 100 pairs were non-overlapping, the minimum start separation was 48.1 seconds, and the resulting 200 clips had unique hashes.

Every View A excerpt searched all 100 View B excerpts, placing its correct match among 99 tracks that were not the same recording. I repeated the search from B to A, producing 200 queries per model. All fifteen embedding models and TempoCNN completed both views with zero failures.

WHAT THE SCORES MEAN

What the paired-section test measures.

A model can pass this test perfectly and still surface other tracks that are obvious, wrong, or useless behind the deck. The paired-section test measures whether a model still recognizes a recording when it hears a different part. A model that loses track identity across sections is a poor foundation for retrieval.

Two low scores need context. CLAP reached 0.485 top-1, but this experiment never tested its intended text-to-audio job. A written query such as “dark broken techno with soft pads” needs a shared text and audio space, and that needs a separate evaluation.

OMAR-RQ multifeature reached 0.120 top-1 while OMAR-RQ base reached 0.865. The multifeature version used a simple pooling and readout step I wrote. That readout may be the failure, so I am not treating the score as a verdict on the model itself.

MERT 330M scored below MERT 95M on top-1, recall at five, and MRR. The larger checkpoint also took roughly twice as long per clip in the first pass. More parameters did not help this task.

TEMPO

TempoCNN is the tempo model I will use.

TempoCNN produced a median raw disagreement of 0 BPM between the two excerpts. Ninety-one percent of tracks landed within 3 BPM after allowing for half-time and double-time readings. Raw Pearson correlation was 0.591; octave-aligned correlation was 0.900.

I then checked both excerpts against the DJ metadata. Eighty-eight tracks had at least two source BPMs that agreed within 3 BPM after octave alignment. Nine conflicting Serato and Rekordbox entries were excluded. Traktor covered only four tracks in this sample.

INTERPRETABLE HEADS

Stable scores still need human meaning.

Seven official Essentia heads ran across both excerpts from all 100 tracks. A head is a small classifier that scores one quality. The seven were approachability, engagement, danceability, instrumentation, mood and theme, voice versus instrumental, and DEAM arousal and valence. All 200 excerpts completed in 349.4 seconds with zero failures. The sample has no human labels for any of those qualities, so every result below measures only whether a score repeats across sections or agrees with another measurement.

Approachability was the most stable score, with a cross-view correlation of 0.950. Danceability reached 0.934, but it labeled 80 of 100 View A excerpts and 79 of 100 View B excerpts as danceable, with medians near 0.99. On a DJ archive that score sits too close to its ceiling to rank tracks. It may still separate obvious cases.

Voice probability reached 0.827 cross-view correlation and 82 percent top-class agreement. Its correlation with Demucs vocal energy share was only 0.443. DEAM valence reached 0.848 and arousal 0.832 across views. Engagement reached 0.829.

Instrument tags had a median per-label correlation of 0.860 and 82 percent top-label agreement. The bass classifier correlated 0.345 with Demucs bass energy share. The drums classifier correlated negative 0.209 with drum energy. Mood and theme tags reached 0.830 median per-label correlation and 75 percent top-label agreement, while the model's published metadata reports a test PR-AUC of 0.14. I keep classifier labels and measured stem energy as separate data. I will not use raw mood tags until a person reviews them.

Candidates

Approachability, voice, DEAM arousal and valence, and selected instrument probabilities go forward to be checked against people's ratings.

Coarse filter

Danceability is stable but sits near its ceiling on this archive. It can separate obvious cases but cannot rank finely.

Hold

Engagement and raw mood or theme tags wait until people's ratings show what the scores mean.

STEMS AND STRUCTURE

Demucs fits the workstation. Separating the whole archive is not practical.

Demucs splits a track into drum, bass, vocal, and other stems. I ran it two ways. First, I separated all 200 thirty-second excerpts in memory and kept only the measurements. Every excerpt completed in 221 seconds with zero failures, and no rendered stems remained. Drum, bass, other, and vocal energy shares reached cross-view correlations of 0.809, 0.741, 0.711, and 0.667. Mean and upper-percentile stem-share change reached 0.789 and 0.757. Threshold peak count reached only 0.308 and was dropped.

A sixteen-dimensional structural vector built from those measurements retrieved the other excerpt from the same track at 0.293 top-1, 0.500 recall at five, and 0.397 mean reciprocal rank across 198 valid queries. Random baselines were 0.010 top-1 and 0.051 recall at five. One nearly silent track was excluded by a fixed input-level rule. The structure vector does recognize the same track, but it trails Discogs-track at 0.975 top-1 and CLAP at 0.485. It can add to an embedding but cannot replace one.

The second trial asked whether stem activity could reveal full-track arrangement changes that a mixture-only analyzer missed. I ran Demucs 4.0.1 with the htdemucs model on twenty complete tracks, two from each benchmark bucket. All twenty finished on the RTX 2080 SUPER. The trial covered 120.5 minutes of music in 7.94 minutes of GPU time.

6.55 GiBpeak total GPU use

I measured one-second activity curves from each stem and compared their transition candidates with a mixture-only detector. A candidate counted as stable only when it survived two-, four-, and eight-second context windows. The mixture detector retained 56.8 percent of its candidates. The stem detector retained 48.3 percent.

The stem-minus-mixture difference was negative 8.4 percentage points, with a 95 percent bootstrap interval from negative 22.6 to positive 5.2 points. Only 39.7 percent of selected stem boundaries matched a mixture boundary within four seconds. Forty-six stem-only candidates survived all three context widths, a median of two per track.

Nobody has marked the real arrangement boundaries in these tracks, so I cannot say whether those extra candidates are correct. Recombined stems tracked the source closely, with a median waveform correlation of 0.9992. That confirms the timing. It does not show that the stems sound cleanly separated.

Use Demucs after selection.For a track chosen for deeper review, derive compact stem-activity measurements and transition candidates, verify the result, then delete the WAV stems.

At the measured speed, processing all 51,302 files in the archive at five minutes each would take about 12.3 GPU days. Keeping the float32 stems would take roughly 19.8 TiB before backups. The runtime is manageable. The storage is not, and the trial showed little added accuracy.

EXECUTION NOTES

Eight gigabytes was enough when the models took turns.

Everything ran locally on an NVIDIA RTX 2080 SUPER with 8 GB of VRAM. Sequential execution avoided memory conflicts, and every candidate fit. The embedding comparison peaked at about 4.46 GiB while OMAR-RQ loaded. Demucs reached 6.55 GiB during full-track separation.

The original pass of all sixteen models over View A took 23 minutes 6 seconds. View B took 23 minutes 13 seconds from the first model start through TempoCNN completion. A fresh paired run therefore costs about 46 minutes on this machine.

I also ran the Discogs model two ways on one test clip: ONNX on the GPU and TensorFlow on the CPU. Their minimum framewise cosine similarity was 0.99999988, and their maximum absolute difference was 0.00000165.

01

The NVIDIA kernel and userspace driver versions were mismatched at the start. One reboot aligned both at 595.84.

02

MERT required a Python 3.11, PyTorch 2.2, and Transformers 4.38 compatibility environment to load every checkpoint key correctly.

03

Essentia's TensorFlow wheel expected old CUDA 11 libraries. Its frozen graphs ran on CPU while the dynamic Discogs ONNX model used CUDA 12.

04

Demucs rejected the documented hybrid-model maximum when passed as 7.8 seconds. An integer seven-second segment ran correctly and completed the twenty-track trial.
